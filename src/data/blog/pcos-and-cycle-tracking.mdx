# PCOS and Cycle Tracking: Navigating Irregularity

Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) is a common hormonal condition that affects how the ovaries work. For those with PCOS, the 'standard' 28-day cycle is rarely the reality. However, tracking remains a powerful tool for self-advocacy and health management.

## How PCOS Affects Your Menstrual Cycle

The primary impact of PCOS is on ovulation. Because the body may not receive the right hormonal signals to release an egg, cycles can become very long (oligomenorrhea) or stop altogether (amenorrhea). This makes traditional calendar-based tracking difficult.

## Why Tracking Matters with PCOS

-   **Identify Ovulation:** By using markers like Basal Body Temperature (BBT), you can see if and when you are actually ovulating, even if your cycle is 50 days long.
-   **Monitor Symptom Severity:** Track how lifestyle changes (like nutrition or exercise) impact symptoms like cystic acne, hirsutism, or fatigue.
-   **Data for Your Doctor:** When you use the [LunaCycle Tracker](/tracker), you have a concrete history to show your physician, which is much more effective than relying on memory.

## What to Look for in Your Data

When tracking with PCOS, look beyond just the dates:
1.  **Cycle Length Trends:** Are your cycles getting shorter or longer over time?
2.  **Temperature Shifts:** Does your BBT rise and stay high for at least 10 days? (This indicates ovulation).
3.  **Cervical Mucus:** Do you see multiple 'patches' of fertile mucus without a period following?

## Working with Healthcare Providers

Bringing your [LunaCycle history](/tracker) to an appointment can help your doctor:
-   Determine if you are ovulating.
-   Decide if further hormonal testing is needed.
-   Evaluate the effectiveness of medications like Metformin or Inositol.
