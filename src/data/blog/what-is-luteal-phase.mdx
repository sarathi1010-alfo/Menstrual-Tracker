## Definition

The luteal phase is the latter stage of the menstrual cycle. It begins the day after ovulation and ends the day before your next period starts. During this phase, the body transforms the ruptured ovarian follicle into a temporary endocrine structure called the corpus luteum, which secretes hormones to prepare the uterus for a fertilized egg.

## How Does the Luteal Phase Work?

Once an egg is released, the empty follicle becomes the corpus luteum. This structure produces large amounts of progesterone and some estrogen. Progesterone signals the uterine lining (endometrium) to thicken and secrete nutrients, creating an ideal environment for an embryo. If the egg is not fertilized, the corpus luteum degrades, progesterone and estrogen levels plummet, and the uterine lining begins to break down, leading to menstruation.

## Real-World Example

If you have a 30-day cycle and ovulate on day 16, your luteal phase spans days 17 through 30 (14 days total). During the first week of this phase (days 17-23), you might feel calm and hungry as progesterone rises. In the second week (days 24-30), as hormones drop, you might experience bloating, mood swings, or acne—classic PMS symptoms.

## Why is the Luteal Phase Important?

The luteal phase is the body's primary mechanism for sustaining early pregnancy. Without adequate progesterone production during this time, the uterine lining cannot support implantation. Additionally, the length of the luteal phase is the most consistent part of the menstrual cycle; while ovulation day can vary wildly, the luteal phase almost always lasts 12-14 days.

## Luteal Phase vs Follicular Phase

| Feature | Luteal Phase | Follicular Phase |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Timing** | Post-ovulation. | Pre-ovulation. |
| **Dominant Hormone** | Progesterone. | Estrogen. |
| **Primary Function** | Prepare uterus for implantation. | Mature an egg for release. |
