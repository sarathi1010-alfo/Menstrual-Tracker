## How Does the Luteal Phase Work?
After the ovary releases an egg during ovulation, the empty follicle that held the egg transforms into a temporary endocrine gland called the corpus luteum. The primary job of the corpus luteum is to secrete progesterone. This hormone is crucial because it stabilizes and thickens the endometrial lining of the uterus, making it a hospitable environment for a fertilized egg to implant and grow. If fertilization does not occur, the corpus luteum naturally breaks down after about 12 to 14 days. When it breaks down, progesterone levels plummet, which triggers the shedding of the uterine lining (menstruation).

## Real-World Example
In a standard 28-day cycle where ovulation happens on Day 14, the luteal phase covers Days 15 through 28. Unlike the follicular phase, which can vary wildly in length depending on stress or illness, the luteal phase is usually very consistent for a given individual, almost always lasting between 12 and 14 days.

## Why is the Luteal Phase Important?
A healthy luteal phase is essential for fertility. If the luteal phase is too short (less than 10 days), the uterine lining may begin to shed before a fertilized egg has a chance to implant, which can lead to early miscarriage or difficulty conceiving. This is known as a luteal phase defect. Additionally, the hormonal shifts during this phase are responsible for premenstrual syndrome (PMS) symptoms.

## Luteal Phase vs Follicular Phase
| Feature | Luteal Phase | Follicular Phase |
|---|---|---|
| Dominant Hormone | Progesterone | Estrogen |
| Timing | Post-ovulation | Pre-ovulation |
| Length | Consistent (12-14 days) | Variable |
